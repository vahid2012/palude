Persian carpet authentication is the discipline that separates a gallery-grade rug from a misrepresented one, and it is the discipline a B2B buyer must understand before importing hand-knotted rugs from Iran. Unlike saffron, where a lab certificate settles most disputes, a rug's authenticity is established by a combination of structural inspection, weave analysis, dye identification and documentary provenance — and the cost of getting it wrong is not a shipment rejection but a reputational one with the buyer's own client base. This guide is the authentication framework we apply to every [hand-knotted 9x12 Persian rug](/export/persian-rug-9x12-hand-knotted/) and antique piece, and it is the framework we recommend buyers apply to any Iranian carpet supplier they evaluate.

## Authentication layer 1: hand-knotted versus machine-loomed

The first authentication question is the most fundamental: is the rug actually hand-knotted? Machine-loomed rugs — produced on power looms in various countries and sometimes sold with "Persian-style" descriptions — are not hand-knotted and do not carry the value or the durability of a hand-knotted rug. The visual difference, to a trained eye, is clear; to an untrained eye, it is not.

The field test for hand-knotted construction is the back of the rug. On a hand-knotted rug, the back shows the individual knots tied around the warp threads, with a slight irregularity that is the signature of hand work. On a machine-loomed rug, the back shows a uniform, regular structure that no human hand produces. The knots on a hand-knotted rug are not perfectly uniform — they vary slightly in size and tension, because each was tied by a human hand. A rug whose back is mechanically uniform is not hand-knotted.

We photograph the back of every rug we ship, alongside the face, and the back photograph is part of the spec sheet. A buyer who does not receive a back photograph with a rug offer is a buyer who cannot verify the most fundamental authentication question.

## Authentication layer 2: knot density (KPSI) counting

Knot density — knots per square inch (KPSI) — is the most-quoted metric in the Persian carpet trade, and it is the metric most often mis-stated. A genuine 400 KPSI Tabriz is a high-density, high-value rug; a "400 KPSI" rug offered at a fraction of the genuine price is almost always a misrepresented 200 KPSI piece or a machine-assisted product.

The KPSI verification method is direct: count the knots along one inch of warp, count the knots along one inch of weft, and multiply. On the back of a rug, the warp threads run vertically and the weft runs horizontally; a counted square inch gives the KPSI. We photograph the counted square inch and attach the photograph to the spec sheet, so the buyer can verify the count independently.

For antique and vintage pieces where the back is delicate and should not be handled more than necessary, we count on the face using the symmetric-versus-asymmetric knot visibility method, and we document the method used. The method is less precise than a back count but is the accepted practice for pieces that should not be turned and handled repeatedly.

The KPSI ranges a buyer should know:

- **Tribal / village rugs (Qashqai, Baluch, Turkmen)**: 100-200 KPSI
- **City workshop rugs (Tabriz, Esfahan, Kashan)**: 200-400 KPSI
- **Fine silk pieces (Qum, Tabriz silk)**: 400-700+ KPSI

A 9x12 rug at 400 KPSI carries roughly 6.2 million knots; at a sustained tying rate of 8,000-10,000 knots per skilled weaver-day, that is 18-24 months of one weaver's working life. This is the structural reason a genuine 400 KPSI rug is priced where it is.

## Authentication layer 3: dye analysis — natural versus synthetic

Dye identification is the authentication layer that separates an antique from a vintage piece and a vintage piece from a contemporary one. Persian carpets were dyed with natural materials — madder root (red), indigo (blue), weld (yellow), pomegranate rind (yellow-brown), walnut husk (brown) — until the introduction of synthetic dyes in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The transition was gradual and varied by region: some weaving centres adopted synthetic dyes earlier than others, and some never fully abandoned natural dyeing.

The dye test is a combination of visual assessment and, for high-value pieces, laboratory dye analysis. Visually, natural dyes produce a depth and a subtle variation (called "abrash") that synthetic dyes do not — natural-dyed wool has a slightly uneven saturation that is the signature of the dye pot, while synthetic-dyed wool is flatly uniform. For high-value antique pieces where the natural-dye claim is material to the value, we commission a laboratory dye analysis that identifies the dye families present and confirms or refutes the natural-dye claim.

The dating implication: a rug presented as "antique pre-1900" should show natural dyes (or, for some regions, very early synthetic dyes that are themselves datable). A rug presented as pre-1900 but showing post-1950 synthetic dyes is mis-dated. The [antique pre-1900 Heriz rug](/export/persian-rug-9x12-hand-knotted/) market is the category where dye analysis most often surfaces mis-dating.

## Authentication layer 4: foundation inspection

The foundation — the warp and weft that the knots are tied onto — is a regional and chronological indicator. Pre-1900 Persian rugs, particularly village and tribal pieces, are typically wool-on-wool (wool warp and wool weft). Cotton foundations became common in city workshop production from the late 19th century and in village production from the early 20th century. Silk foundations appear in fine Tabriz and Qum workshop pieces and in some silk prayer rugs.

The foundation inspection is performed on the back of the rug, at the edge where the warp and weft are visible. Cotton foundation is white and uniform; wool foundation is off-white to grey and shows the texture of spun wool; silk foundation is glossy and fine. The foundation material is recorded on the spec sheet and is one of the indicators, alongside dye analysis, that support an age opinion.

For the **silk foundation wool pile Persian rug** — a common Tabriz construction where silk warp and weft give crisp drawing and durability, and wool pile gives warmth and cost — we document the foundation/pile split explicitly, because it materially affects both value and care instructions.

## Authentication layer 5: age verification and provenance

Age verification is the synthesis of the previous layers. A genuinely old rug shows: natural dyes (or regionally appropriate early synthetics), a foundation consistent with the region and period, a handle and patina that comes from decades of washing and walking (the wool softens in a way that a new rug's wool does not), and a drawing style that matches the region's documented evolution. For high-value antique pieces we commission a written age opinion from an independent Iranian appraiser and attach it to the export documentation.

Provenance — the documented history of the rug's ownership — is the layer that turns an authentic rug into a collectible. A rug with a documented chain of ownership (a gallery, a private collection, an exhibition) carries a premium over a structurally identical rug with no provenance. For antique pieces we document whatever provenance is available, and we are honest about what is not.

## The Cultural Heritage export permit

The export of antique rugs from Iran is subject to the Iranian Cultural Heritage Organization's review. Pieces above a defined age threshold require an export permit, and certain categorically protected pieces cannot leave the country at all. This is not a workaround-able regulation; it is enforced at customs, and a rug seized for lacking a permit is not returnable. We handle the permit application as part of the sourcing workflow and will not quote a piece for export that we cannot legally ship. A buyer who is offered an antique rug without a Cultural Heritage permit is a buyer who is being offered a rug that cannot legally leave Iran.

## The documentation set for an authenticated carpet

Every Palude carpet export ships with:

- **Certificate of Origin** from the Iran Chamber of Commerce, naming the weaving city/region.
- **Cultural Heritage export permit** where the piece's age requires it.
- **Independent appraisal / age opinion** for antique and high-value pieces.
- **Spec sheet** with dimensions, KPSI, materials, age and condition.
- **Photographic set**: face, back, detail, and any restoration.

## Putting it together

Persian carpet authentication is a multi-layer discipline — hand-knotted verification, KPSI counting, dye analysis, foundation inspection, age opinion, provenance — and a buyer who skips any layer is a buyer who eventually absorbs the cost of a mis-represented piece. The buyers who succeed treat authentication as a documented process, with a spec sheet, a photographic set, and (for antiques) a written age opinion, all assembled before the rug ships.
