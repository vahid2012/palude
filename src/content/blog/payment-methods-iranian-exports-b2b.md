Payment is the step in Iranian import trade where the most first-time buyers stall, and it is the step that should be resolved before a contract is signed, not after. Iranian banks are not directly connected to the European or US clearing systems, which means a direct EUR or USD payment from an international buyer to an Iranian supplier account is generally not possible. The working payment channels exist — they are used every day by importers across the EU, Gulf, CIS, South Asia and East Asia — but each channel has its own requirements, timelines and bank-confirmation prerequisites. This guide covers the channels that work, the steps to confirm a channel with your bank, and the documentation each channel requires. It is the payment companion to our [Iran export documentation checklist](/blog/iran-export-documentation-checklist/).

## Why Iranian payments are routed through third countries

The structural reason Iranian payments require routing is that Iranian banks are not part of SWIFT's standard clearing for most international currencies, following the re-imposition of secondary sanctions in 2018. (The exact SWIFT status of individual Iranian banks has shifted over time; the practical assumption is that a direct EUR or USD payment to an Iranian bank will not clear.) The consequence is that Iranian export payments are routed through correspondent banks in third-country jurisdictions — jurisdictions whose banking systems clear with both the buyer's country and Iran — and the payment is structured as a payment to the third-country correspondent that is then onward-transferred to the Iranian supplier.

This is not a workaround; it is the established trade-finance mechanism for Iranian exports, used by European, Gulf and Asian importers for food, petrochemical, stone and carpet shipments every day. The third-country correspondent is typically a bank in a jurisdiction that maintains trade-finance relationships with both sides — the specific jurisdictions change as sanctions designations evolve, and we advise on the current working correspondents at the point of contracting.

## Channel 1: third-country correspondent bank transfer

The most common payment channel for Iranian exports is a third-country correspondent bank transfer. The mechanics:

1. The buyer's bank sends the payment to a correspondent bank in an agreed third-country jurisdiction.
2. The correspondent bank receives the payment and onward-transfers it to the Iranian supplier's bank.
3. The supplier receives the funds in Iran, in the agreed currency (EUR, RMB, or local currency depending on the correspondent).

The timeline is typically 3-7 business days from the buyer's bank sending the payment to the supplier receiving the funds, depending on the correspondent and the destination. The cost is the buyer's bank's outgoing transfer fee plus the correspondent's handling fee, typically a combined 0.1-0.5% of the payment value.

The prerequisite: the buyer's bank must confirm, before the contract is signed, that it will process a payment to the agreed third-country correspondent. Some European banks decline Iranian-related payments regardless of the correspondent jurisdiction; others process them routinely. The confirmation is a phone call to the buyer's trade-finance desk, not a written policy lookup — banks' internal Iranian-payment policies are not always documented publicly.

## Channel 2: letter of credit (LC)

The letter of credit is the channel large buyers use for first-time relationships and high-value shipments, because it protects both sides. The mechanics:

1. The buyer's bank issues an LC in favour of the Iranian supplier, payable against the shipping documents (commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin, product-specific certificates).
2. The LC is confirmed through a third-country correspondent bank that adds its confirmation undertaking to pay.
3. The supplier ships the cargo, presents the documents to the confirming bank, and the confirming bank pays the supplier against the documents.
4. The buyer's bank reimburses the confirming bank and collects from the buyer.

The LC's protection is documentary: the supplier ships against the agreed document set, and the buyer pays against the confirmed shipment. The LC is the channel we recommend for first-time [Iranian polyethylene film grade supplier](/export/iranian-polyethylene-film-grade/) and [Persian travertine marble supplier](/export/persian-travertine-marble-supplier/) relationships, where the shipment value is high and the relationship is new.

The cost of an LC is the issuing bank's fee plus the confirming bank's fee, typically a combined 0.5-2% of the LC value depending on the banks and the tenor. The timeline is 7-14 days from LC issuance to confirmation, depending on the correspondent.

## Channel 3: escrow and counter-trade

For established relationships and specific jurisdictions, an escrow arrangement or a counter-trade (goods-for-goods) structure is the working channel. Escrow: a third-party escrow agent holds the buyer's funds and releases them to the supplier against the agreed document set or against the buyer's confirmation of goods received. Counter-trade: the buyer supplies goods or services to the Iranian counterparty in exchange for the Iranian export, with the values balanced in a structured transaction.

These channels are not for first-time buyers; they are for relationships where the trade pattern is established and the parties have the infrastructure to manage a non-standard payment structure. We use escrow and counter-trade selectively and only where the buyer's and supplier's profiles support them.

## The currency question: EUR, RMB, or local

Iranian export payments are denominated in EUR, RMB (Chinese yuan), or — increasingly — the currencies of Iran's major trading partners (INR for India, RUB for Russia, AED for the UAE). USD payments are generally not used because USD clearing exposes the payment to US secondary sanctions jurisdiction. The currency choice is a buyer-supplier negotiation based on which currency the correspondent bank handles most efficiently and which currency the supplier can use most readily in Iran. For European buyers, EUR is the default; for South Asian buyers, INR or EUR; for Russian and CIS buyers, RUB or EUR; for Chinese buyers, RMB.

## What to confirm with your bank before contracting

The confirmation a buyer should obtain from their bank before signing an Iranian import contract:

1. **Will the bank process a payment to the agreed third-country correspondent?** This is the foundational question; a "no" here means the contract cannot proceed with this bank.
2. **What documentation does the bank require to process the payment?** Banks' Iranian-payment documentation requirements vary — some require the full shipping document set, others require only the commercial invoice and a payment-purpose declaration.
3. **What is the bank's fee and the estimated correspondent fee?** This is a landed-cost line item; it should be known before the contract is priced.
4. **What is the expected payment timeline?** This determines the working-capital cycle for the buyer and the cash-flow expectation for the supplier.
5. **Does the bank's compliance team require any pre-clearance for Iranian-related payments?** Some banks require a compliance review that adds 2-5 business days to the first payment; this is not a problem if it is planned for, and is a problem if it is discovered at the payment stage.

We walk buyers through this confirmation before we issue a proforma. A buyer who contracts before confirming the payment channel is a buyer who may discover, at the payment stage, that their bank will not process the transaction — and the shipment stalls, with the cargo sitting at the Iranian loading port accruing storage.

## Payment timing: advance, against documents, or on arrival

The payment-timing structure is a buyer-supplier negotiation:

- **Advance payment** (100% before shipment): the supplier's preferred structure; exposes the buyer to non-performance risk. We do not recommend advance for first-time relationships.
- **Against documents** (payment on the shipping documents being presented): the LC structure, or a documentary collection. The standard for first-time and mid-value relationships.
- **On arrival** (payment after the cargo arrives and is inspected): the buyer's preferred structure; exposes the supplier to non-payment risk. Used only in established relationships with a documented trust history.

The structure we default to for first-time buyers is the LC or a documentary collection against the shipping documents — the structure that protects both sides and that the documentation workflow supports.
